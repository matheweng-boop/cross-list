Charging in and around the car: USB-C power delivery, DC-DC, BMS and chargers, and what the Qorvo power ICs would need

28 Oct 2026 · Preliminary report from the owner's three comments of 28 Oct 2026: (a) “Full charging solutions using Qorvo Power Management ICs - also thinking outside of the box for BMS and Car chargers? What would it take to close this gap?” (b) “DCDC with USB C for Automotive?” (c) “Making own PD Controller as a new product?” Built from BizDev data (Qorvo and Skyworks catalogues with the AEC-Q rule, the path-to-product data, 4,195 public reference designs, the ACT88321 review) and public web sources. It builds on hot-swap at 400 V and 800 V and the case isolated current sensing for EV charging. “est” = our estimate. Every figure and link: background notes.

In brief

  1. (b) DC-DC with USB-C for cars: yes, as a buck-boost (est, med). A car USB-C port needs a 4-switch buck-boost, because the 12 V battery sags to about 6 V in a cold crank and rises to 16 V and above, while the port must give 5–20 V (3.3–21 V with PPS). Qorvo has one automotive PD-ready part, the ACT4751M buck (4.5–40 V in, 4 A, written to supply a USB PD controller). A buck cannot make 20 V from 12 V, so from a 12 V battery it reaches about 9 V × 3 A = 27 W per port (est); 60–100 W needs a buck-boost we do not have in automotive grade.
  2. The market has gone to one chip per port. TI TPS25762-Q1 / TPS25772-Q1 (PD + 65 W buck-boost, Grade 1), Infineon EZ-PD CCG7D / CCG7S (PD 3.1 + buck-boost controller, up to 100 W per port), Richtek RTQ7880, MPS MPQ5031-AEC1, ADI MAX25432; Weltrend sells automotive-grade PD controllers (WT6617F, WT6633Q), Microchip the UCS4002 port protector. Our data hold 37 automotive USB-C reference designs; none uses our parts.
  3. (c) Our own PD controller: no, not as a standalone product (est, med). It needs a certified PD 3.1 stack and firmware, a CC PHY, VBUS and VCONN switches, short-to-battery protection and AEC-Q100, in a market with at least eight automotive suppliers and consumer controllers listed at a few cents. Partner first; license a PD engine later for a one-chip ACT buck-boost.
  4. (a) Full charging solutions: we already hold the isolation side (est, low). Automotive Skyworks isolated gate drivers (Si8239x, Si828x), isolators (Si86xx), isolated power (Si88xx), isolated current and voltage sensing (Si89xx) and the Si875x contactor driver fit BMS, on-board chargers and charging stations. Gaps: a BMS cell-monitor AFE with ISO 26262 (Qorvo's PAC25140 is a 10–20-cell BMS SoC, not automotive), OBC controllers and SiC / GaN power stages (UnitedSiC went to onsemi in January 2025).
  5. Closing the gap: build the automotive buck-boost (medium effort, est) and a 48 V BMS from PAC2514x (medium); partner for the PD controller, the BMS AFE with ASIL D and SiC; do not build OBC controllers or power FETs. Sell the isolation set now through the current-sense case.

  • Market structure: crowded. At least eight suppliers sell automotive PD controllers or one-chip ports (TI, Infineon, Richtek, MPS, ADI, Microchip, Weltrend, and Renesas with an MCU design); in consumer chargers, Chinese controllers are listed at a few cents on trade sites (snippet, low). Average selling price of a standalone automotive PD controller: Not Enough Data; we expect it to be low and falling (est).
  • Where value sits: in the one-chip port (PD + buck-boost + protection), where the power stage carries the watts and the EMI work, and in the system (power sharing between ports, thermal derating). A standalone PD controller is the commodity half (est).
  • Build vs buy vs partner (est): build alone: long, certification-heavy, low price; buy: a small PD-controller company would bring the stack and USB-IF experience, but adds little beyond the controller; partner: pair our automotive buck-boost with a partner's certified automotive PD controller in a joint reference design, as Microchip and Renesas do with two chips. Recommended: partner now, license a PD engine into the ACT buck-boost later, if the first wins hold.
  • Our assets: Active-Semi's PD-ready buck line (ACT4751 / ACT4752 with PD 3.0 PPS output control, ACT4529 with QC 2.0, ACT4751M automotive), ACT power stages with integrated FETs (ACT5101 / ACT5102 buck-boost, ACT2861 charger), Arm MCUs and mixed-signal blocks in PAC SoCs. Skyworks isolation matters only on the 48 V and high-voltage side, not for a 12 V USB port.

variant · path data: usb_pd_controller = variant, medium effort, “build - extend ACT475x”; they list the same missing pieces: CC / PD 3.1 engine and firmware, USB-IF certification, sink-side controller.

Other gaps in the car USB-C chain

  1. An automotive 4-switch buck-boost, 100 W class. From ACT5101 / ACT5102 (consumer, 4–23 V) and ACT4751M (automotive, 40 V): a 40 V input, 3.3–21 V output, PPS-grade 20 mV / 50 mA steps, spread spectrum, AEC-Q100 Grade 1. TI and Richtek show it can sit next to the PD logic in one die.

    variant · path: pre_regulator = variant, medium effort, “build - Qorvo ACT power line” (missing: 40 V automotive buck-boost, AEC-Q100 + load dump, low-EMI spread spectrum).

  2. Port protection (short-to-battery on VBUS, CC, D+, D−). A small analog part next to any PD controller; Microchip sells it alone (UCS4002).

    variant · from the PLP eFuse silicon (path: efuse_hotswap = variant, short effort).

  3. Input protection (ideal diode, reverse battery). Commodity; leave to partners.

    gap, not recommended · path: reverse_polarity has no catalogue match.

For the decision: The buck-boost is ours to build; the PD controller is better bought in through a partner, because its cost is in certification and firmware, not in silicon we already have.

3. Where else we see opportunities

(a) “Full charging solutions”: BMS, on-board chargers and charging stations

  • 12 V and 48 V BMS. Lithium 12 V batteries and 48 V mild-hybrid and zonal packs need cell monitoring (4 cells at 12 V, about 13–14 at 48 V, est), balancing, a disconnect switch and isolated or galvanically separated communication. TI's BQ79616-Q1 family (16S, ASIL D, ±1.5 mV) has a standalone 48 V monitor (BQ75614-Q1). Qorvo's PAC25140 / PAC25141N (10–20 cells, Arm MCU, CAN, 145 V) is the nearest asset, built for e-bikes and tools (est), not automotive. Our isolators and Si89xx sensing fit the high-voltage pack (BMU to vehicle, pack current); contactor and pre-charge drive is the hot-swap report.
  • On-board chargers 11 / 22 kW and the 800 V → 12 / 48 V DC-DC. Three-phase or totem-pole PFC plus a CLLC or DAB stage on 1200 V SiC (Wolfspeed and ST designs, snippets); controlled by an MCU or DSP. We supply the isolated gate drivers (Si8239x, Si828x with desat), isolators, isolated bias (Si88xx) and isolated voltage and current sensing (Si89xx, see the current-sense case). Not ours: the controller, the power switches, magnetics.
  • EV charging stations (wallbox, DC fast charger). The same isolation, drive and sensing set, industrial or automotive grade; at 1000–1500 V DC the extra-wide isolation case applies.
  • Outside the car: e-bikes and power tools (PAC25140, ACT2861), power banks (ACT28xx, mostly discontinued), USB-C chargers and docks (ACT4752, ACT5101). Consumer grade is enough there.

4. Market trend and who is doing it

  • Trend: USB-A to USB-C, 15–27 W to 60–100 W per port, two to six ports per car (rear seats, head units), PD 3.1 with PPS; 140 W and DisplayPort in head units (TI PMP31344); EPR at 28–48 V and 240 W is defined by USB-IF but not yet seen in car ports (search, Not Enough Data).
  • Integration: from PD controller + buck-boost + protector (Microchip, Renesas, MPS) to one chip per one or two ports (TI, Infineon, Richtek).
  • Value: automotive USB-C and USB PD market estimates disagree more than tenfold (about $16 M to $3.2 B for 2025, analyst snippets), so none is used. Ports per car: Not Enough Data.
  • Charging chain: OBCs move to 11–22 kW, bidirectional, on 1200 V SiC; BMS AFEs to ASIL D with redundant measurement; 48 V zonal power adds packs and DC-DC in every car (est).

For the decision: The port market rewards one-chip integration; a buck-boost we build plus a partner's PD controller is a second-source story, not a lead, until we can integrate.

Answers to the three questions

  • (b) DC-DC with USB-C for automotive? Yes, as a buck-boost (conf med). ACT4751M proves the automotive 40 V buck; an automotive 4-switch buck-boost at 100 W is the missing block. Next: ask the power BU for its feasibility.
  • (c) Our own PD controller as a new product? No, not standalone (conf med). Crowded, low price, long certification and firmware work; partner now and license a PD engine for a one-chip version later if the buck-boost wins. Whether Active-Semi's PD firmware still exists: Not Enough Data.
  • (a) Full charging solutions, BMS and car chargers: what would it take? (conf low) Sell the Skyworks isolation set now; build a 48 V BMS monitor from PAC2514x and the 800 V auxiliary flyback (medium, est); partner for the high-voltage BMS AFE, OBC control and SiC. A full charger from our parts alone is not reachable without SiC and controllers.

For the decision: One build (the buck-boost), one partnership (PD controller) and one sell-now set (isolation, drive, sensing) answer the three comments.

Risks

  • One-chip ports (TI, Infineon, Richtek) may leave no socket for a separate buck-boost, even as a second source.
  • A partner PD controller makes our part depend on another firm's firmware and certification timing.
  • The ACT line is consumer- and data-centre-focused; only ACT4751M and ACT4529M are automotive today, so qualification is a new effort for each block.
  • Thin data: Qorvo's own pages are not read (rules); MPS, Microchip, ADI and NXP figures are snippets; market sizes disagree; Active-Semi PD firmware status unknown.

For the decision: Test the buck-boost with the BU and one Tier-1 before any money goes to a PD controller.
