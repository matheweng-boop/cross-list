Hot-swap at 400 V and 800 V in the car: pre-charge, DC arcs, solid-state switches and where our isolation fits

23 Oct 2026 · Research topic asked by the owner: “I would like a research topic for hot-swap controllers in automotive. Are there any known pain points in having to get near 800 V and be able to safely plug something in or out?” · built from BizDev data (parts database, Skyworks catalogue, block-diagram library, library topic on traction-inverter isolation, cached Skyworks app notes AN1052 and AN1456) and four public TI pages read on 23 Oct 2026. Standards are paid and were not read: they are summarised at the level the owner asked for. Our own reasoning is marked “est”. All sources: background notes.

In brief

  1. Short answer: yes, there are well-known pain points, and the car solves them by never really hot-plugging at 400–800 V. Before anything is plugged in or out, the high-voltage system is switched off by contactors, the connectors are interlocked, and the capacitors are pre-charged when it is switched back on. The “hot-swap controller” of a 12 V or 48 V board does not exist at 800 V; its job is done by contactors, pre-charge circuits, fuses and, more and more, solid-state switches.
  2. The pain points: (1) inrush: connecting 800 V to the inverter's or charger's capacitors without pre-charge would draw kiloamps (about 16 kA with 50 mΩ in the loop, est); (2) DC arcs: unlike AC, an 800 V DC arc does not go out by itself, so opening a connector or a relay under load burns it; (3) contactors that weld, wear and are big and costly; (4) insulation faults must be found before someone touches the car (insulation monitoring); (5) the capacitors must be discharged quickly after switch-off or a crash.
  3. The trend: solid-state parts take over pieces of the mechanical chain: SiC-MOSFET active pre-charge (TI reference design TIDA-050063), solid-state relays for insulation monitoring (TI TPSI2140-Q1, 1200 V), and solid-state battery disconnects and eFuses on the 48 V and zonal side (est). Each solid-state switch on the high-voltage side needs an isolated driver with its own floating supply, plus isolated current and voltage sensing.
  4. Where we fit: our Si875x isolated FET driver builds exactly such solid-state relays without a floating supply (AN1052), but only at 2.5 kVrms in a narrow SOIC-8; TI's equivalent, TPSI3050-Q1, is 5 kVrms reinforced, 1000 Vrms working, automotive, $2.30 at 1k. Around it we have the full set: isolated gate drivers with fast short-circuit detection (Si828x, Si829x), isolated current and voltage sensing (Si89xx), isolators (Si86xx) and isolated bias (Si88xx).
  5. Data centres (the owner's earlier remark): the same problem appears in 48 V racks, where hot-swap controllers and eFuses are mature, and in the new 800 V DC racks (NVIDIA from 2027), where boards really are plugged in live; TI already shows an “800 V hot-swap controller” in its data-centre line-up. The car and the data centre need the same building blocks (est).
  6. Next step (feasibility 2): a reinforced, automotive successor of Si875x (5 kVrms, SSO-8, higher gate drive, fault feedback) sold as a solid-state pre-charge and disconnect set with our gate drivers and Si89xx sensing, for 800 V cars and 800 V DC racks.

1. Adjacent products and technologies

The high-voltage “hot-plug” chain in a car is: contactors (or solid-state switches) + pre-charge + interlock + insulation monitor + discharge, each with a controller on the 12 V side and a barrier in between. Every place where a switch on the high-voltage side is driven from the 12 V side needs an isolated driver, and every place where current or voltage is watched needs isolated sensing: that is our isolation line.

  • 48 V racks: server boards and power shelves are hot-plugged every day; hot-swap controllers and eFuses are mature there (TI, ADI, Infineon and others; the library's battery-backup diagram has an ORing / hot-swap block: BBU). Not isolated, so not our field.
  • 800 V DC racks: NVIDIA moves its AI racks to 800 V DC from 2027; TI's announced 800 V line-up includes an “800 V hot-swap controller” next to its 800 V to 6 V isolated converter.   • Battery storage racks (1000–1500 V): rack contactors, pre-charge and insulation monitoring as in the car; TI's 1500 V rack monitor reference (TIDA-010272) uses the TPSI2140 relay (rack BMS).

3. Market trend and technology

400 V and 800 V architectures

Most EVs use a 400 V class battery (about 250–450 V); a growing share uses 800 V class (about 550–900 V) for faster charging and thinner cables, usually with SiC in the inverter. A fully charged “800 V” pack sits near 900 V, and fast chargers go to 1000 V. Every high-voltage component has capacitors on its input, so every switch-on is a capacitor charge (est, common knowledge; not researched in this run).

Inrush and pre-charge

Example (est): 800 V into 500 µF of DC-link capacitors stores 160 J (½·C·V²). With only 50 mΩ of battery, cable and contactor resistance in the loop, the first current peak would be about 16 kA. A 50 Ω pre-charge resistor limits it to 16 A, charges the link in about 75 ms (three times R·C), and must absorb the same 160 J every time, which is why pre-charge resistors are large and why a fault (a short behind the contactor) can burn them. Active pre-charge with a SiC switch and an inductor (TI TIDA-050063) replaces the resistor and the pre-charge contactor with a controlled current, which needs an isolated driver on the high-voltage side.

Arcing at DC connectors and contacts

AC current passes zero 100–120 times a second and an arc usually dies there; DC never does. At 400–800 V an arc across a few millimetres keeps burning until the gap is long enough or the arc is blown out. That is why HV connectors are never meant to be opened under load (HVIL makes sure of it), why contactors have magnetic blow-out or gas-filled chambers and a limited number of breaks under load, and why welding of contactors after an inrush or short is a known failure (est, common engineering knowledge; not researched in this run).

eFuses and hot-swap controllers

A hot-swap controller limits the inrush when a board is plugged into a live bus and switches it off on a short; an eFuse puts the switch and the controller in one part. Both exist up to about 80 V (12 V and 48 V cars, 48 V racks) and are not isolated. Above that, the same job is built from discrete SiC switches, an isolated driver, isolated sensing and a controller: a “solid-state circuit breaker” (est). TI announced an 800 V hot-swap controller for data centres (eeNews Europe, Mar 2026); details not read.

Isolation monitoring

The high-voltage system floats against the chassis, so a single insulation fault is not dangerous yet; the car must find it before a second one makes it so. An insulation monitor switches known resistors between the high-voltage rails and the chassis and measures the voltage shift; the switches must stand the full voltage plus test pulses. TI builds this with TPSI2140-Q1 (1200 V, 50 mA, 2 mA avalanche for HiPot and charger surge tests) and isolated amplifiers (TIDA-01513, TIDA-010232).

Safety standards (summary; not read)

  • ISO 6469-3 (electrically propelled road vehicles, electrical safety): requirements for voltage class B circuits (above 60 V DC / 30 V AC up to 1500 V DC / 1000 V AC): protection against electric shock (basic insulation plus barriers and enclosures, or double / reinforced insulation), equipotential bonding, minimum insulation resistance (commonly quoted: 100 Ω/V for DC circuits, 500 Ω/V for AC), and marking.
  • LV 123 (German carmakers' test specification for HV components) became ISO 21498 (Part 1: voltage sub-classes and characteristics; Part 2: electrical tests for components), including the behaviour at switch-on and switch-off and the discharge of the component's capacitors.
  • Related: UN ECE R100 and FMVSS 305 (post-crash: high voltage below 60 V DC or low energy within a short time), IEC 61851-23 for the DC charger side, ISO 26262 for the functional safety of the switching chain.
