What it is

Creepage is the shortest path along the package surface between the two sides of the barrier; clearance is the shortest path through air. Wide-body SOIC and SSO-8 packages offer about 8 mm, extra-wide packages (TI DWW, Novosense "W") 14.5–15 mm. Creepage protects against slow surface tracking at the steady working voltage; clearance protects against fast sparks from short voltage spikes.

What sets the minimum

Three things, per IEC 60664-1 and the end-product standard: the working voltage, the pollution degree of the air around the part, and the material group of the insulating surface. Reinforced insulation needs twice the basic distance.

Pollution degree: how dirty and damp it gets

DegreeIn plain wordsTypical places
PD1No dirt, or only dry dirt that does not conduct.Sealed or potted parts, boards under a qualified conformal coating, clean rooms.
PD2Dirt does not conduct, but now and then condensation makes it conduct for a while (usually when switched off).Offices, labs, closed enclosures and cabinets, household appliances; the default for most isolator datasheets and most sealed car and charger housings.
PD3Dirt that conducts, or dry dirt that becomes conductive because condensation is expected.Machine tools and factory floors, unheated rooms, farming; the outside of outdoor inverters and chargers.
PD4Lasting conduction from conductive dust, rain or snow.Open outdoor equipment, under and on top of rail vehicles.

A housing with at least IP54 may let the parts inside be designed for a lower pollution degree (CONEC note on IEC 60664-1); outdoor solar inverters are typically certified PD3 outside and PD2 inside an IP65 box.

Material group: how well the surface resists tracking

The comparative tracking index (CTI, IEC 60112) is the highest voltage at which a material survives 50 drops of salty water without forming a conductive track. The higher the CTI, the shorter the creepage may be.

Why it matters: group I mould compound halves the distance against group III. At 800 V and PD2 a group I package needs 4 mm (basic), a group III one 8 mm. The board under the part is usually FR4 (group IIIa), so the board needs the larger value or a slot.

  • Reinforced = 2 × basic. Example: 800 V, PD2, group I: 4.0 mm basic, 8.0 mm reinforced (TI's on-board charger example).
  • DC: the working voltage is the highest rms or DC value across the barrier, so an 800 V DC link uses the 800 V row. Values in between may be interpolated linearly (TI used 565 V DC → 2.8 mm, PD2 group I).
  • Clearance follows the impulse (spike) voltage instead: from the mains voltage and overvoltage category (OVC I–IV), reinforced one impulse step higher, times an altitude factor above 2000 m (1.29 at 4000 m, 1.48 at 5000 m). Example: 230 V mains, OVC II needs 2.5 kV basic (1.5 mm) and 4 kV reinforced (3.0 mm). For circuits without mains spikes, TI cites "nominal voltage + 1200 V". For a wide-body or extra-wide package, creepage is usually the tighter limit.
  • Some product standards use their own tables (IEC 62368-1 Table 17, IEC 61800-5-1 Table 10, IEC 62109-1 Table 14); the values above are the common IEC 60664-1 ones.

When it deviates

Less package creepage can make the part unusable at your voltage even if the electrical ratings match. Ways designers avoid a wider package: conformal coating or potting (PD1, qualified per IEC 60664-3), a sealed housing (PD2), placing the safety barrier elsewhere (functional isolation at the driver, basic plus earth, reinforced only where people can touch), two isolators in series (needs an extra supply in the middle), and slots in the board (at least 1 mm wide in PD2, 1.5 mm in PD3) so the board keeps the distance the package has.

How to judge it

Ask for the working voltage (DC link or highest rms), the pollution degree and basic or reinforced. Read the table above for material group I and compare with the package. Then check the board too: FR4 is group IIIa, so keep slots or keep-outs under the part.
