Example part: Skyworks Si88x2x, a dual digital isolator with an on-chip DC-DC controller that drives a small external transformer. Everything here is public data or general physics; points marked est are BizDev's own reasoning.

The one idea: where does the common-mode current return?

Both tests are about common-mode current: current that flows the same way on all wires and comes back through stray capacitance and the reference ground plane. In the EFT burst test the generator pushes it in; in the CISPR emissions test the product's own DC-DC converter makes it, and the receiver measures how much of it reaches the cables. In an isolated design the current has to cross the isolation barrier somewhere. It takes every capacitance it finds:

  • the isolator itself: 1.4 pF input-to-output (Si88x2x data sheet, Table 8, at 1 MHz);
  • the DC-DC transformer's interwinding capacitance: typically several to tens of pF, depending on the transformer est, so usually far bigger than the isolator's 1.4 pF;
  • any Y (safety) capacitor or overlapping ground planes the designer adds across the barrier;
  • and the stray capacitance of each side, its load and its cables to the ground plane (Cret,A, Cret,B).

AN1131 puts it plainly: it is often the unintentional common-mode currents that contribute most to emissions, and in its Si88241 case study the transformer interwinding capacitance is the dominant cause of conducted emissions. Change any of these paths, in the test setup or in the design, and the result changes.

What the isolator must survive. The fast edge that the barrier sees must stay inside the isolator's common-mode transient immunity: Si88x2x CMTI 40 kV/µs minimum, 100 kV/µs typical (data sheet, Table 6). A rough way to compare setups est:

barrier slope ≈ (Cinj / (Cret,A + Cret,B + Cacross)) × Vburst / trise

Cinj is how strongly the burst couples into the circuit, Cacross the sum of everything across the barrier. 1 kV in 5 ns is 200 kV/µs at the generator, five times the CMTI minimum, so how much of it reaches the barrier matters. Use it to compare setups, not to predict pass or fail: the stray capacitances must be measured or simulated, and wiring and choke inductance slow the real edge. EFT burst (IEC 61000-4-4): setup choices

Per IEC 61000-4-4 (summary): 5/50 ns pulses into 50 Ω, in bursts at 5 kHz or 100 kHz repetition, burst period 300 ms; on power lines the coupling/decoupling network (CDN) injects through 33 nF coupling capacitors, in common mode against the reference ground plane. The product standard sets the test level and whether a temporary glitch is acceptable.

All lines together, or N only

  • All lines (L + N + PE together) is mainly a common-mode stress: the whole primary side jumps against the ground plane and the current returns through the barrier capacitances and the secondary side's stray capacitance.
  • N only adds a line-to-line part: the input filter, rectifier and regulator are stressed as well, not only the barrier. If the hit line is tied to one side's ground (for example GNDB on the secondary) while that side's supply has no high-voltage path of its own, most of the slope lands across the isolator est.

Coupling capacitance: 33 nF, or 3 × 10 nF in series

Three 10 nF capacitors in series are 3.3 nF. Where they sit decides what they mean:

  • In the generator's coupling path instead of the standard 33 nF, they make a milder test. With 50 Ω the time constant is about 165 ns against a 50 ns pulse, so the peak drops only a little, but the charge each pulse can push into a low-impedance input falls (about 40 % less in BizDev's simple model est). This is not a valid standard test.
  • In the product, across the barrier, the same 3.3 nF is a design choice (stitching or Y capacitance): a local return path that lowers the barrier slope in EFT and lowers emissions in CISPR, at the cost of more leakage current at mains frequency. These capacitors must be safety-rated (Y class); three in series is a common safety choice.

Filters

Filters only on L and N, with none on the ground path, change little: the common-mode current simply takes the unfiltered ground path. A common-mode filter helps only if every path the current can take goes through it.

DC-DC on or off

  • With the converter running, the transformer's interwinding capacitance is a coupling path far bigger than the isolator's 1.4 pF, and the burst current flows into the converter's secondary rail: it can droop or reset.
  • The converter also adds its own emissions (next section).
  • Testing with it off, or with the secondary fed from a bench supply, is not representative: the path and the failure that matter are not exercised.

CISPR 25 / CISPR 32 emissions: setup choices

  • Networks. CISPR 25 (vehicle components) feeds each supply lead through a 5 µH / 50 Ω artificial network (AN); an AN is needed on the return lead too if it is longer than 200 mm. CISPR 32 (multimedia equipment) measures mains through a 50 µH / 50 Ω LISN. The network sets the impedance the common-mode current sees, so results from one do not carry over to the other.
  • Harness and probe positions. In the CISPR 25 current-probe method the probe clamps the complete harness, at 50 mm and at 750 mm from the product. Harness length and height over the plane change the result.
  • Classes. CISPR 25 has classes 1 to 5 (5 is the strictest), agreed between purchaser and supplier in the test plan. CISPR 32 has Class A and the stricter Class B (residential).
  • Bonding. Whether the secondary ground, the load or a shield is bonded to the plane decides whether the converter's common-mode current goes back through the measured network or stays local. A bond that the real installation does not have makes the result meaningless est.
  • Load and DC-DC load. The converter's switching current, and with it the emissions, depend on the load it supplies. Test at the real (or worst-case) load, with the converter running.
  • The converter. Si88x2x switches at about 250 kHz (225–275 kHz, data sheet Table 6); Si883xx/6xx can be set between 200 and 800 kHz. The data sheet says the converter uses dithering for low EMI. Its harmonics fall right inside the 150 kHz-and-up range both standards measure.

Design levers and their trade-offs

LeverWhat it doesCost of it
Y / stitching capacitance across the barrier, near the transformer Gives the common-mode current a short local return path. AN1131: a single 1000 pF Y capacitor let a 2-layer Si88241 board pass CISPR 25 class 1 and 2 conducted limits. Also lowers the barrier slope in EFT est. More leakage current at mains frequency (safety standards limit it); must be Y1/Y2 rated (AN1131); its own inductance limits it above some tens of MHz. Overlapping inner ground planes (4+ layers) give a capacitor without a part.
Snubber Damps ringing and voltage spikes on the switch and rectifier. The data sheet recommends one to minimise radiated emissions; in AN1131 the secondary snubber gave a few dB at high frequency for about 1 % efficiency. A resistor and capacitor; a little efficiency.
Common-mode choke High impedance for common-mode current while passing the supply current. AN1131 found a choke on the converter's input gave the greatest benefit in its case study. Board space and cost; it stops working above its self-resonant frequency, so ferrite beads or a capacitor may be needed as well.
Layout and transformer Short switching loops that do not run through the ground and power planes (AN1131); a transformer with lower interwinding capacitance (in AN1131, ten turns of tape between windings instead of one cut it by about 33 %). Design effort, not parts; possibly a 4-layer board.

AN892 (the Si882xx/883xx DC-DC design guide) covers the converter itself: an asymmetric half-bridge flyback, with a transformer leakage inductance kept small (its example: under 100 nH) and a rectifier diode chosen with margin above the calculated reverse voltage, because the diode capacitance and the leakage inductance add a voltage spike.

Testing to pass versus testing right

These changes make a result invalid (they test something other than the product):

  • a coupling capacitance other than the standard 33 nF in the EFT generator path (for example 3 × 10 nF in series);
  • DC-DC converter off, or the secondary powered from a bench supply;
  • a bond, shield or ground connection in the test that the real installation does not have, or one left out that it has;
  • a harness, network (5 µH vs 50 µH), probe position or load other than the test plan says;
  • a class not agreed in the test plan.

These are legitimate design changes (keep them, and re-test the full product): Y or stitching capacitance across the barrier (with the leakage-current check), a snubber, a common-mode choke covering every path, a better layout or a lower-capacitance transformer.
