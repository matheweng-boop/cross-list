Part knowledge: isolated current sensing · capacitive digital isolators · isolated gate drivers

Offset and gain: two kinds of error

  • Offset is the same at every reading: the output is wrong by a fixed amount, even at zero. It is given in mV or µV at the input. To compare it with other errors, divide it by the full-scale input: then it is a % of full scale (absolute error). At one tenth of full scale the same offset is ten times larger as a % of the reading.
  • Gain error scales with the signal: the slope is a little wrong. It is a % of reading (relative error); at zero input it is zero, at full scale it is largest.
  • Nonlinearity is the bend away from the best straight line, given as % of reading or full scale. A two-point calibration (zero and full scale) removes offset and gain at that temperature, but not nonlinearity.

Skyworks AN1270 adds the three as percentages after turning the offset into % of the 250 mV full scale of the Si8922B (AN1270 p. 9). That is exact at full scale; at small currents the offset share grows.

From a 25 °C value to a band over temperature

Data sheets give each error at 25 °C plus a drift per °C. The error at another temperature is the 25 °C value plus drift × (T − 25 °C), keeping the sign: −40 °C is −65 °C away (AN1270 p. 8). With a minimum and a maximum for both the value and the drift there are four lines; the outermost at each temperature form the band (the "four-line method", AN1270 p. 7).

Worst case or root-sum-of-squares

  • Straight sum (worst case). Add the limits. It is right when the errors are correlated and reach their limits together (for example two drifts caused by the same temperature, or an error that appears twice in a chain). It guarantees the limit, but is usually too pessimistic for independent errors.
  • Root-sum-of-squares (RSS). √(a² + b² + c²). It is right when the errors are independent: the standard deviation of a sum of independent variations is the RSS of their standard deviations (AN1270 p. 12; the "law of propagation of uncertainty", NIST).

For the Si8922B over −40 to 125 °C: straight sum −0.858 % / +0.816 %, RSS −0.530 % / +0.484 % (AN1270 p. 12, Table 6). AN1270 recommends RSS as the best estimate of the total static error (p. 13). Correlated parts of a budget are summed first and the result is then combined by RSS with the independent parts (est).

Typical, minimum, maximum: what the numbers mean

  • Typical is roughly the average part at the stated conditions. It is not a promise; about half the parts are worse (est).
  • Minimum / maximum are limits the maker tests in production or guarantees by design and characterisation. For a normal distribution, limits at ±3σ hold 99.73 % of parts; makers often set limits wider than that (est for the margin makers use; NIST for the 99.73 %).
  • To use a limit in a statistical (RSS) budget, turn it into a standard deviation: a normally distributed limit at ±3σ gives σ = limit / 3; a limit with no information about the spread is treated as rectangular, σ = limit / √3 (NIST, Type B evaluation).
  • An uncertainty band is then the combined standard deviation times a coverage factor: k = 2 for about 95 % confidence, k = 3 for more than 99 % (NIST).

Uncertainty bands in practice

  1. Initial error at 25 °C: offset, gain, nonlinearity, plus the shunt tolerance and the ADC.
  2. Over temperature: add drift × ΔT from 25 °C to each, with its sign (four-line method).
  3. Over life: add the ageing the data sheet states (shunt resistors drift too); where none is stated, say so and keep a margin (est).
  4. After calibration: a two-point calibration at 25 °C removes the initial offset and gain error of that board; what remains is drift × ΔT, nonlinearity, noise and the calibration's own uncertainty (est).
  5. Combine correlated parts by straight sum, independent parts by RSS, and state the confidence (k).

The same thinking for timing across a digital isolator

  • Propagation delay (tPHL, tPLH) shifts every edge; it matters when a signal goes out and back (an SPI read).
  • Pulse-width distortion (PWD = |tPHL − tPLH|) shortens or lengthens each pulse.
  • Channel-to-channel skew is the delay difference between channels of one part; part-to-part skew (tPSK) between two parts, which matters when, for example, the high-side and low-side signals use different isolators or drivers.

Isolated SPI, from AN1359 (p. 4-8). With the Si86SS51 (PWD < 3 ns, skew < 3 ns, delay 9 ns), a write cycle loses PWD + skew = 6 ns of setup and hold time. A read loses 24 ns, because the clock goes out and the data comes back through two more delays (PWD + skew + tPHL + tPLH). That limits a normal SPI clock to 10 MHz. An asymmetric clock shares the delay between read and write (15 ns lost, 20 MHz); looping the isolated clock back so the controller samples with it removes the absolute delay (6 ns lost, 40 MHz). Timing limits move together with temperature and supply, so a timing budget is usually a straight sum of the limits (est).

Gate drive. In a half bridge the dead time must cover the worst-case difference in delay between the high-side and low-side paths plus the transistor's turn-off time. On the Si823x the delay is 30 ns typical and 45 ns maximum, with PWD below 5.6 ns (data sheet p. 23); between two separate parts the mismatch can approach the spread between their fastest and slowest delay, which is why one dual driver (matched channels) or a stated part-to-part skew is preferred (est). Deglitch filters add a fixed delay (30 ns on the Si86Sx deglitch options, AN1359 p. 13) and, if asymmetric, distortion (AN1462 p. 1).
