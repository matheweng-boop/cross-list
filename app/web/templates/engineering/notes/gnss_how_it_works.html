How positioning works

  1. Each satellite says where it is and what time it is. It sends a ranging code (a known pseudo-random sequence) and a navigation message with its orbit (ephemeris) and clock correction. Satellite clocks are atomic and steered to system time (Navipedia, GNSS signal).
  2. The receiver measures travel time. It lines up its own copy of the code with the received one; the shift is the travel time, about 67-90 ms from a GPS satellite. Times the speed of light gives a range of roughly 20 000 km.
  3. It is a pseudorange. The receiver's clock is a cheap crystal, so every measured range carries the same unknown clock error: 1 µs of clock error is 300 m on every satellite at once.
  4. Four unknowns, four satellites. Position x, y, z plus the receiver clock offset: four unknowns need at least four satellites. More satellites are combined in a least-squares fit, which also shows when one measurement is wrong.
  5. Geometry matters (DOP). If the satellites are bunched together in one part of the sky, their range "circles" cross at shallow angles and a small range error moves the position a lot. Dilution of precision (DOP) is that magnification factor: position error ≈ DOP × range error. Satellites spread over the sky, with some low on the horizon and one overhead, give a low DOP (Navipedia, Positioning Error).

NavIC also uses an S-band signal at 2492 MHz.

  • Why the bands pair up. Systems share centre frequencies on purpose (L1 = E1 = B1C, L5 = E5a = B2a) so one antenna and one front-end path receive several constellations.
  • Protected and less protected. 1559-1610 MHz and 1164-1214 MHz are also allocated to aeronautical radio navigation, so no other users may interfere there; L2 and E6 (1215-1350 MHz) are shared with ground radars and are more exposed (Navipedia, GNSS signal).
  • Why L1 + L5 removes the ionosphere. The ionosphere delays the code by 40.3 · TEC / f²: it depends on the frequency. Measuring the same range on two frequencies gives two equations with one common unknown (TEC), so the delay can be calculated and removed (Navipedia, Ionospheric Delay). L1 and L5 are far apart in frequency, which makes the correction strong; the price is about 2.6 times more noise in the combined range (est from the standard ionosphere-free combination).

  • SBAS (WAAS, EGNOS, MSAS, GAGAN): geostationary satellites broadcast clock, orbit and ionosphere corrections and integrity on L1 / L5; about 1 m.
  • RTK: a base station a few km away sends its measurements; differencing cancels almost all common errors and carrier phase gives centimetres, once the integer ambiguities are fixed.
  • PPP / PPP-RTK: precise orbit, clock and bias corrections from a service (over cellular or satellite, e.g. Galileo E6 HAS, QZSS L6, BeiDou B2b) give decimetres to centimetres anywhere, after a convergence time.
  • Dead reckoning with an IMU and wheel speed bridges tunnels and garages; the library topic quotes 7 cm (triple frequency) against 124 cm (single) horizontal rms with PPP + IMU on a low-cost antenna.

Caveats

  • Urban canyons: few visible satellites, high DOP, reflections instead of direct signals.
  • Jamming and spoofing: the signal is far below noise, so a small jammer overwhelms it; spoofers send fake signals. Defences: interference monitoring (AGC, C/N0), multi-band, authenticated signals (Galileo OSNMA), IMU plausibility checks.
  • Interference from in-car radios: LTE / 5G uplink, Band 13 / 14 harmonics on L1, V2X and Wi-Fi in the same fin (see GNSS front-end parts).
  • Antenna placement: roof or fin over a ground plane; dashboard or mirror positions lose sky view.
  • Time to first fix: a cold start must download the orbits (up to about half a minute or more); hot starts with stored ephemeris and assistance data (A-GNSS) take seconds.
