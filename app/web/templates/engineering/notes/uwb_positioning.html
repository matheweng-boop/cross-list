Four ways to find a position

UWB sends pulses about 2 ns long and timestamps them very precisely. Light travels 30 cm in a nanosecond, so a good timestamp is a good distance. The same radio can use those timestamps in four ways:

Two-way ranging (TWR)

How far? Two devices swap messages and time the round trip. Car keys, door locks, item finding.

Time difference of arrival (TDoA)

Where in the building? One message, several synchronised anchors compare arrival times. Asset tracking, indoor navigation.

Lightweight TDoA {{ est|safe }}

The lightest tag. Our reading: TDoA where the tag only sends one short blink. Not a standard name; see its section.

Phase difference of arrival (PDoA)

Which direction? Two antennas on one receiver compare the phase. With the TWR distance, one anchor gives a position.

1 · Two-way ranging (TWR)

The tag sends a poll. The anchor answers with a response after a reply time that it measures with its own clock. The tag measures the whole round trip with its clock; round trip minus reply time is twice the flight time. That is single-sided ranging (SS-TWR). A third message, the final, gives both sides a round trip and a reply time: double-sided ranging (DS-TWR).

Why the third message: each crystal is a few ppm off (IEEE 802.15.4a allows up to ±20 ppm). In single-sided ranging the error is about c × reply time × (difference of the two crystal errors) ÷ 2: a 400 µs reply and 15 ppm between the two crystals give 90 cm. The asymmetric double-sided formula
ToF = (Tround1·Tround2 − Treply1·Treply2) / (Tround1 + Tround2 + Treply1 + Treply2)
leaves only the distance × the average crystal error (25 µm at 10 m and 2.5 ppm), whatever the two reply times are. The plain average of the two round trips cancels the clock error only when both reply times are equal.

When to use it

  • Something that opens or unlocks: a phone or key fob ranges to the car or door. CCC Digital Key and Aliro door locks use double-sided TWR, secured by the 802.15.4z STS (Samsung Research on FiRa 4.0).
  • Finding one item with a phone: distance from TWR, direction from PDoA.
  • Few tags, no fixed infrastructure: any two devices can range; anchors need no synchronisation.
  • Avoid for hundreds of tags at high rates or coin-cell tags that must last years: every fix costs three or four messages per anchor and the tag has to receive.

What it costs. A 2-D position needs at least three anchors (one more than the dimensions). A study with DWM1000 radios counted 4 messages per anchor when the tag starts (3 when the anchor starts), so 12 messages per 2-D fix and about 10-12 fixes per second for one tag, because an anchor needs about 500 µs to reply (Ramesh et al.). One poll answered by all anchors in turn cuts this to N + 2 messages {{ est|safe }}. The tag's receiver is on in every exchange and receiving draws 72-88 mA on a DW3000 (our DW3000 review), so a TWR tag at one fix per second lasts months on a coin cell, not years {{ est|safe }} (see the lightweight TDoA visual). On one channel, a few hundred tags at one fix per second {{ est|safe }}.

2 · Time difference of arrival (TDoA)

The tag sends one short message, a blink. Every anchor that hears it notes the arrival time. Nobody knows when the tag sent it, but the difference between two arrival times is known, and it puts the tag on a hyperbola: the line where the distance to one anchor minus the distance to the other stays the same. Three anchors give two hyperbolas that cross at the tag (2-D); four give 3-D or a spare. GPS works the same way, with satellites as anchors (see GNSS: how satellite positioning works).

The catch: the anchors' clocks must agree to a fraction of a nanosecond, because 1 ns of sync error is 30 cm of range difference. Crystals drift apart quickly (two DWM1000 nodes drifted 26 ns per second in one test), so anchors are kept in sync continuously, over cables or by sync messages from a reference anchor (Ramesh et al.).

UL-TDoA (uplink)

  • The tag only sends short blinks; the anchors listen.
  • Very low tag power: no receiver and no maths on the tag.
  • One message per fix, so it scales to many tags.
  • Needs synchronised anchors and a backhaul (Ethernet or wireless) to a location server, which computes and knows every position.
  • Standardised in FiRa Core 4.0 (Dec 2025): "UL-TDoA tags" tracked by "UL-TDoA anchors".

DL-TDoA (downlink)

  • The anchors send in turn; the tag only listens and computes its own position, like a GPS receiver.
  • Any number of tags: they never transmit.
  • Privacy: the infrastructure does not learn who is there.
  • The tag's receiver must be on for the anchors' messages, which costs more than a blink; the update rate is set by the anchors' round (Ramesh et al.).
  • Used for indoor navigation of phones and robots (MobileKnowledge FiRa kit).

When to use it

  • Many tags in a fixed area (UL-TDoA): pallets, tools, carts, workers, patients' equipment, players. Factories, warehouses, hospitals, arenas.
  • Phones or robots that find their own way without being tracked (DL-TDoA): indoor navigation in shops, airports, venues.
  • Avoid where nobody will install and synchronise anchors, or where only the distance to one device matters (car key: use TWR).
  • Accuracy: MobileKnowledge quotes 90 % of fixes within ±20 cm (UL-TDoA) and ±10 cm (DL-TDoA), one 4-anchor cell per 500 m²; a DWM1000 test put 92 % within 20 cm in an 8 m room with a sync message every 100 ms (Ramesh et al.).

3 · Lightweight TDoA {{ est|safe }}

What we found. "Lightweight TDoA" is not a defined method name in the public material we checked: the FiRa Core 4.0 announcement, Samsung Research's summary of FiRa 4.0, IEEE 802.15.4ab documents found by search, three arXiv papers and vendor pages. The FiRa and IEEE specifications themselves are for members or paid, so we could not check their text.

Closest use of the word: FiRa 4.0's UL-TDoA, in which a device "periodically send[s] a lightweight uplink message" and the UL-TDoA anchors estimate its location; the tags stay simple, run on coin cells and need no computing power for the location (Samsung Research). FiRa's own release says the design "optimizes tag simplicity and power consumption".

Open question for the owner: Which document or product used "lightweight TDoA"? With it we can replace our reading below by its definition.

Our reading {{ est|safe }}: lightweight TDoA is TDoA with the tag cut to the minimum. It wakes, sends one short blink and sleeps again: no listening, no ranging, no maths. Anchors, sync and server stay as in UL-TDoA, so the hyperbolas above do not change; what changes is the tag's battery, its cost and how the tags share the air. Other possible readings: DL-TDoA (the tag only listens), or TDoA whose anchors synchronise over the air instead of by cable.

Classic TDoA tag {{ est|safe }}

  • Also listens: for its time slot, commands or new settings.
  • Scheduled slots: no collisions, but every tag must hear the schedule.
  • Tag and anchors usually from one vendor's system.
  • Battery in our model: a few months at one fix per second, because the receiver is on in every fix.

Lightweight TDoA tag {{ est|safe }}

  • Only transmits: one short blink per fix, never listens.
  • Random access: no schedule to hear; with many tags some blinks collide and that fix is skipped.
  • Interoperable tags and anchors: FiRa 4.0 certification covers UL-TDoA.
  • Battery in our model: about two years at one fix per second; a simpler, cheaper tag.
  • Nothing can be sent back to the tag (no change of rate or settings on the fly).

When to use it

  • Many cheap tags that only need to be found: pallets, tools, containers, equipment.
  • Battery life comes first and an occasional skipped update does not matter.
  • Avoid when the tag must know its own position (DL-TDoA), must receive commands quickly, or needs every single update (use a scheduled, two-way tag).

4 · Phase difference of arrival (PDoA)

A receiver with two antenna ports about half a wavelength apart hears the same pulse twice. Arriving from the side, the signal travels an extra d·sin θ to the far antenna, which shows as a phase difference Δφ = 2π·d·sin θ / λ. The chip measures Δφ and works back to the angle, θ = arcsin(λ·Δφ / (2π·d)) (Margiani et al.). The wavelength is 46.2 mm on channel 5 (6489.6 MHz) and 37.5 mm on channel 9 (7987.2 MHz); half of it, 23.1 mm or 18.8 mm, is the usual spacing.

  • Ambiguity: the phase repeats every 360°. With d above λ/2, two or more angles give the same reading, so the spacing stays at or below λ/2, or a third antenna is added.
  • Accuracy falls off to the sides: straight ahead the phase changes fastest with angle; towards ±90° it hardly changes, so the same phase noise gives a much larger angle error. A two-antenna DW3220 node was linear within ±45°, above 5° average error around ±60° and of little use beyond, with 2.4° average error in a 90° range around the centre (Margiani et al.).
  • Front or back: two antennas cannot tell whether the signal comes from in front or behind; the angle only holds within ±90°.
  • One anchor gives a position: TWR distance r plus angle θ: x = r·sin θ, y = r·cos θ. The error across the line of sight is r × the angle error: 5° at 5 m is about 44 cm {{ est|safe }}.
  • Needs STS: on the DW3000 the internal antenna switch for PDoA only runs on 802.15.4z packets with an STS (Margiani et al.; our DW3000 review), which quotes PDoA ±10° and an angle of about ±5°.

When to use it

  • Item finding: the phone shows an arrow to the tag (distance from TWR, direction from PDoA).
  • Car access: one anchor with two antennas tells which side of the car the key is on; a vendor claims fewer anchors per car {{ est|safe }}.
  • Point and control: pointing a phone at a lamp, speaker or door.
  • Avoid for tags far off to the side (beyond about ±60°) or behind the antenna pair, unless more antennas or anchors cover them; calibrate the phase offset between the two receive paths on every board.
