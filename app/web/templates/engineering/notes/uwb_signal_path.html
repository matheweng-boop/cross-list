The one idea: the receiver times the first edge of a 2 ns pulse

A UWB key measures distance by time of flight: 1 ns is 30 cm. The pulse spreads over the whole 499.2 MHz channel and is only about 2 ns wide. In a car the direct path to the key often goes through a body or a seat, so it arrives weaker than a reflection a few nanoseconds later. The receiver must find that weak first tap in front of the strong ones. Anything in the transmit or receive chain that changes the shape of the pulse changes where its first edge appears:

  • Too late: the first path is smeared into the strong reflection or lost in noise, and the distance comes out long (an accuracy problem).
  • Too early: a small copy of the strong reflection appears before it (a pre-echo) and is taken for the first path. The key looks closer than it is, which is the direction an attacker wants (a security problem; library rule d11).

The owner's rule (library d12, confirmed 23 Oct 2026): preserve the TX and RX pulse across the full spectrum so the UWB shape is maintained. A weak signal or first tap is more exposed to echoes, noise and interference, so the signal path must stay phase-linear and the amplifiers must stay out of compression, so that they add no interference of their own.

Why phase linearity: group delay and its ripple

Group delay is the time each frequency takes to pass a part: τg = −dφ/dω, the slope of the phase of S21. If it is the same at every frequency in the channel (linear phase), the pulse is only delayed, and a fixed delay is removed by the antenna-delay calibration. If it varies across the 499.2 MHz, different parts of the pulse arrive at different times and the pulse is distorted.

A useful way to see it is the paired-echo rule (Wheeler, 1939): a ripple in group delay of peak size ΔGD that repeats every fp across frequency acts like two small copies of the pulse, one before and one after the main pulse:

echo spacing = ±1 / fp  ·  echo size ≈ ΔGD · fp / 2 (relative to the pulse)

The echo is relative to each path, including the strongest. If the first path is 15 dB below a reflection, a −20 dB pre-echo of that reflection is only 5 dB below the true first path and can sit in front of it {{ est|safe }}. Amplitude ripple (gain not flat across the channel) makes paired echoes in the same way.

Where it comes from: filter skirts (sharp BAW/SAW filters have group-delay peaks near their band edges), narrow or high-Q matching networks, mismatch between parts (two reflections a line length apart make an echo at twice the line delay, of size |Γ1|·|Γ2|), and long or poorly matched cables between the antenna and the anchor. A constant delay offset is calibrated out once; its change with temperature, channel and part-to-part spread is not, and becomes a ranging bias: 100 ps is 3 cm {{ est|safe }}.

Why amplifier linearity: low compression

  • Strong near signals. A key at 30 cm is 30–40 dB stronger than one at 10 m. If the LNA or the receiver's input compresses on the strong taps, it clips them: the energy spreads in time and frequency, the sidelobes rise and can cover the weak first path. A bypass mode for strong signals (as in QM14068) is a linearity tool, not only a power saver.
  • Blockers. 6 GHz Wi-Fi 6E/7, 5.9 GHz V2X and other radios in the car reach the UWB front end; a non-linear LNA mixes them into the channel as interference. The ETSI anchor must keep working with a −50 dBm CW interferer (library d5).
  • Transmit side. The power is capped at −41.3 dBm/MHz, so a UWB PA such as QM14070 only makes up losses; run it well below its compression point so the transmitted pulse keeps its shape and meets the standard's pulse mask (minimal precursor, which the secure-ranging receivers assume; library d12, r4).

e.g. QM14070		 Only enough gain to make up the losses to the −41.3 dBm/MHz cap; plenty of back-off from P1dB; flat gain and phase across the channel. Pulse shape at the output (precursor, ringing) against the 802.15.4z pulse mask; output spectrum against the emission mask.
Matching, baluns, cables Broadband, low-Q matching; short, well-matched lines between parts so that reflections do not add echoes. Return loss of each interface over the channel; cable length (each echo appears at twice its delay).

A rule of thumb to start from {{ est|safe }}: keep the whole chain's group-delay variation inside the channel to a few hundred picoseconds peak-to-peak, so that paired echoes stay at least 30 dB below each path, and its gain flat to within about 1 dB. Check it on the cascade, not part by part: ripples of several parts can add.

How to verify: measure S21 of the complete chain with a vector network analyser and read group delay with a smoothing aperture of about 10–20 MHz; or take the inverse FFT of S21 to see the impulse response and look for anything before the main peak. Repeat at hot and cold, and on channel 5 and 9.
